One Equation, Not Two Questions

The Avalanche Foundation published the topic breakdown of its Call for Research Proposals, and its Lead Economist read it as two questions converging. Recluster the Foundation's own categories and it looks closer to one equation, with the staking ratio as the joint unknown.

1 September 2026 · 12 min read · by Meriç Kalkan

The setup

On 31 August 2026 the Avalanche Foundation published a breakdown of its Call for Research Proposals. Over 160 proposals were submitted, roughly 95 progressed to the selection committee, and the Foundation released a chart of what the submissions were about along with per-area word clouds of the research questions themselves.

Eric Lu, Lead Economist at the Foundation, added a reading of that chart:

What stands out to me: two thirds of submissions are converging on the same two questions independently. That's not because we told researchers what to think, it's because those are genuinely the two places the field doesn't have settled answers yet.

Eric Lu, Lead Economist, Avalanche Foundation, 31 August 2026

Two claims sit inside that: first, that these are two questions, and second, that the convergence on them was independent. Both are testable against material the Foundation has already published. This post does that, using the released chart, the released word clouds, and the text of the original April 2026 call.

The conclusion is not that the reading is wrong. It is that the published data supports something sharper.

The published distribution

Seven categories, captured 20 August 2026, summing to 100 percent:

The 34 and 32 at the top are where the two thirds figure comes from. The problem is the other five rows.

The categories are not orthogonal

Emission policy and security budget is not a separate question from validator economics. It is the same question asked from the treasury side: what does the network pay for security. Security-economics tradeoffs is the same question asked from the margin: what does the network get for what it pays.

On the other side, ecosystem and L1 and RWA value accrual is asking where value shows up, and market microstructure and price formation is asking how it gets priced. Both are components of a valuation model, not alternatives to one.

By that argument only one row is methodologically distinct. Mechanism design and formal verification is about proving properties of a system, not estimating a value in it.

Recluster on that basis. The grouping below is mine, not the Foundation's, and the point of showing the arithmetic is so you can disagree with the grouping and still check the sums:

So it is not 66 percent on two questions. It is roughly 91 percent on one equation, and 9 percent on how to prove things about it.

The shared variable

The reclustering above is an argument. The evidence for it is in the Foundation's own call text, which puts the same variables on both sides of its own dividing line.

Area 1 asks about "the role of monetary policy and token emission schedules in determining asset prices and expected returns". Area 2 asks about "optimal staking ratios" and "non-inflation-based reward mechanisms". Emissions and the staking ratio are named under both areas. Not similar topics under each: the same two levers, described once from the holder's side and once from the validator's.

That is the tell, and it comes from published text rather than from any reading of a graphic. Two areas that were genuinely separable would not be sharing their central variables in the document that defines them.

The word clouds are consistent with this, for what that is worth: staking ratio appears in both, and the emissions pair shows up as EMISSION SCHEDULES in Area 1 and REWARD MECHANISMS in Area 2. I treat that as corroboration rather than evidence, for reasons set out below.

One thing to be clear about before going further: the sorting into areas is the Foundation's own, done after applications closed. Submissions were assigned to the two areas defined back in April, and a cloud was generated per bucket. The areas are not clusters that emerged from the data. They are bins, and the clouds describe what is in each bin.

What these clouds can and cannot show

A fair objection: a word cloud is a designed graphic, so why treat it as data at all. Three things say the vocabulary is machine-extracted rather than art directed.

The clouds are full of bigrams cut across phrase boundaries: SCHEDULES FEE, MECHANISMS SUSTAIN, PARAMETERS EMISSION, CONDITIONS AVALANCHE, IMPLIED OPPORTUNITY, PREDICT VALIDATOR. Nobody letters "SCHEDULES FEE" onto a graphic on purpose. Area 1 carries the same pair in both directions, NATIVE POS and POS NATIVE, which is what naive bigram counting does. And Area 2 shows unlemmatised duplicates sitting next to each other, OPPORTUNITY COST with OPPORTUNITY COSTS, REQUIRED RETURN with REQUIRED RETURNS. A designer would have merged those. A script would not. The header also carries a filter predicate, SCORE >= 60.

What that supports is the vocabulary: these terms really do come out of the submission text. What it does not support is precise weighting. Cloud layout engines adjust type size to fill space, and the red and grey split may be an editorial choice rather than a threshold. So every size judgement below is my reading of the graphic, not a measurement, and I have kept the argument off it.

None of the load-bearing claims here rest on the clouds. The 44 / 47 / 9 split comes from the bar chart's own labelled percentages, and the shared-variable argument comes from the call text. The clouds are illustration.

VariableRole in Area 1 (valuation)Role in Area 2 (validator economics)
EmissionsDilution. A cost borne by holders.Reward. The revenue that pays for security.
Staking ratioFloat removed from the market.The quantity of security purchased.
Fee burnSupply removed, so value accrues to holders.Nothing. Fees are burned, so they are never validator income in the first place.

Read that way, each row is the same quantity carrying opposite signs for the two audiences. Set the staking ratio too low and the security budget thins. Set it too high and the opportunity cost of locked capital has to be paid in issuance, which dilutes the very holders the token is meant to accrue value to. I find it hard to construct a case where you answer one area properly without having implicitly answered the other, which is what the reclustering is trying to capture.

My reading

The chart counts proposals, not researchers: over 160 submissions, not 160 people. And on my reading they did not converge on two open questions. They converged on the two unknowns of a single equation, with roughly one submission in eleven working on the proof technique instead.

To be explicit, since this is the load-bearing claim of the piece: the 91 percent grouping is my analytical interpretation of the Foundation's categories. It is not a conclusion the Foundation published.

On independence

The second claim is that this convergence happened independently. Here the original call text matters, because the Foundation wrote the two areas before anyone submitted anything.

The Call for Research Proposals, published on avax.network in April 2026, scoped exactly two areas: Area 1, Cryptoasset Pricing and Valuation, and Area 2, Validator Economics and Network Security. Under each it named four topics of interest. Here is Area 2, verbatim:

We are particularly interested in work that explores optimal staking ratios, the economics of opportunity cost and required returns for validators, metrics for assessing validator set health and decentralisation, and non-inflation-based reward mechanisms that support sustainable long-term outcomes.

Call for Research Proposals, April 2026

Every one of the eight named topics turns up in the published clouds. I want to be honest about how much that is worth, because when I first laid it out it looked like a finding and it is not one. The Foundation wrote a call asking about staking ratios and validator returns, sorted the responses into that call's own two areas, and generated a cloud per area. The call's vocabulary coming back out the other end is close to mechanical. The table below is worth reading as confirmation that the pipeline did what you would expect, not as a discovery:

Eight for eight, then, but mechanically so. That is still worth establishing, because it rules something out rather than in.

There are two explanations that fit these clouds equally well. One is that researchers wrote toward a call that told them what would be funded. The other is that the Foundation named those two areas because they are the field's open questions, in which case the call and the submissions are both downstream of the same reality and the overlap is not evidence of anything. Nothing in the published data separates those.

It is worth saying which part of Lu's reading survives all of this, because most of it does. His substantive claim is that these are the two places the field lacks settled answers, and everything I checked supports that: the ACP traffic, the fee-burn design, the post-Etna split in validator economics all point at exactly those two gaps. The narrow objection is only to the number offered as evidence for it.

That objection is this: the 66% cannot distinguish independent convergence from a well-specified call. It only shows that submissions cluster in the two largest categories. That is a confounded measurement, so it is the wrong place to look for a signal, whichever explanation you favour. Worth adding that the clouds are drawn only from submissions scoring 60 or above, so they describe what the Foundation rated well, which is another reason not to read them as an unfiltered picture of the field.

What I thought the independent signal was, and why I no longer think so

I had an argument here that I liked, and it does not hold. I am leaving it in with the objection attached, because the way it fails is more useful than the claim was.

The argument: the clouds contain things the call never asked for. Area 1's topic list does not mention fee burn, yet BURN RULES, FEE BURNING and FEE MECHANISMS are all in the cloud. Area 2's list does not mention the post-Etna L1 transition, yet AVAX BOND, ACP SHIFT, CONTINUOUS FEE and VALIDATOR CHURN are. Add the 9 percent on formal verification, not a named area, and that looked like the researchers' own contribution showing through.

Why that fails

The call's topics are categories, not keyword lists, and they are broad enough to subsume all of it.

Area 1 explicitly invites "novel approaches to value accrual that extend beyond network usage alone". Fee burn is a value accrual mechanism, arguably the most obvious one on this chain. Area 2 asks how validator incentive systems are "structured, measured, and optimised", which covers post-Etna L1 validator economics comfortably, and stretches far enough to cover mechanism design too.

So every term I wanted to call unprompted can be read as a direct response to the call. I cannot separate "the researchers raised this themselves" from "the researchers answered a broad topic specifically", and neither can anyone else from the published data.

That leaves the honest position: the word clouds cannot carry an argument about independence in either direction. They are a designed graphic with no published frequency data behind them, sorted into bins the Foundation defined, described by topics broad enough to absorb almost anything in the field. What they are good for is showing which specific sub-topics researchers reached for, and that is a description, not an inference.

Which is why the rest of this piece leans on the bar chart's labelled numbers and the call text instead. Both are published, exact, and quotable.

Why the timing is not academic

There is a reason validator economics is the largest bucket in 2026 specifically, and it is visible in the Area 2 cloud in terms like ACP SHIFT, AVAX BOND, CONTINUOUS FEE and NON-INFLATIONARY VALIDATOR.

The Etna upgrade, which activated ACP-77 in December 2024, removed the 2,000 AVAX bond for L1 validators specifically. They now pay a continuous fee instead, base rate near 1.33 AVAX per validator per month, and they are no longer required to sync and validate the Primary Network. The ACP does not put that rate under a governance vote: it says a future ACP can adjust the parameters, which means another protocol upgrade rather than an on-chain decision.

This distinction matters and is easy to blur. Primary Network validators were not changed: they still stake a 2,000 AVAX minimum and still earn staking rewards. What Etna did was create a second class of validator that sits outside the emissions loop entirely, paying a cost to operate rather than drawing issuance for it. Two validator economies now run side by side under one token, which is precisely the kind of thing that generates research questions.

Since then the direction has continued. ACP-285 would reduce the MinConsumptionRate governing Primary Network staking rewards from 10 percent to 7.5 percent, on the argument that the floor functions as untargeted issuance inside the reward formula, ramping linearly over 90 days rather than stepping. Its formal status is still Proposed. It is bundled into Helicon, which activated on the Fuji testnet on 28 July 2026, and mainnet activation had not been announced at the time of writing. Modelling puts the effect at roughly 0.5 to 1 percentage point off annual inflation, pulling the minimum-duration annualised reward rate from about 5.4 percent toward 4 percent and widening the premium for staking longer.

The surrounding numbers are what make this urgent rather than theoretical. Roughly 43 percent of AVAX supply is staked at a headline rate near 6.7 percent, and the security budget is funded entirely by issuance against a fixed 720 million supply cap.

Why "just use fee revenue" does not work here

On Avalanche, transaction fees are burned. Both the base fee and the priority fee are destroyed rather than paid to whoever produced the block, which is a deliberate difference from Ethereum.

So fee growth is not validator income. Burning offsets issuance in supply terms and accrues to holders, but a validator sees none of it, however busy the chain gets. Retiring issuance therefore cannot be paid for out of fees under the current design. It needs either a new mechanism that routes value to validators, or validators accepting a lower required return. Foundation economists have themselves floated a zero-inflation validator model along these lines. Nobody has published a credible number for what either path costs, which is exactly the hole this research programme is pointed at.

BENQI put the same point more compactly in a reply under the Foundation's thread on 31 August 2026, noting that "non-inflation-based reward mechanisms" is a polite way of saying the current emission schedule has a clock on it. Worth noting that the phrase they are quoting is the Foundation's own, lifted from the call text, which is what makes the observation land. I have not been able to link that reply directly, so it is cited here as what it is: a reply visible in the thread, quoted as read. The reading itself is right, and it is why the cost-of-security side is 44 percent of the funnel once you stop splitting it across three rows.

A note on the funnel

Over 160 submissions to roughly 95 progressing is an advance rate near 59 percent, and probably a little under it given the "over". The Foundation has not published what the screen tested for, so this is worth reading carefully rather than confidently.

What the surrounding structure suggests is a scope-and-quality floor rather than a tournament. Applications ran on a rolling basis, grants go up to 50,000 USD, and disbursement is staged at 30 percent on award, 30 percent at a six-month review and 40 percent on a publishable final paper. Grantees are also invited to present at the third Crafting the Cryptoeconomy conference at Columbia in autumn 2026. That is a programme built to seed a literature, not to pick a single winner.

The committee composition points the same way. It has six members: Emin Gün Sirer of Ava Labs, Matias A., the Foundation's Chief Investment Officer, Eric Lu, its Lead Economist, Campbell Harvey of Duke and a former President of the American Finance Association, Fahad Saleh of the University of Florida and Columbia's Center for Digital Finance and Technologies, and Agostino Capponi, who directs that centre. That is a committee assembled to referee for publication, not to run a hackathon.

What the residual should produce

If the headline number is confounded, the value of this programme sits in what the rest of it produces. Three things I would want to read, and have not:

  • A joint model where the staking ratio is endogenous rather than a parameter, so that valuation and security clear at the same time instead of being solved in sequence.
  • An empirical estimate of validator exit elasticity with respect to yield, using the post-Etna L1 fee cohort as a natural experiment. The bond removal is a clean structural break and it is now nearly two years old, which is enough data.
  • A costed comparison of the two exits from issuance: a mechanism that actually routes value to validators, against validators simply accepting a lower required return. Since fees are burned rather than paid out, those are the only two doors, and the security budget debate is still being argued qualitatively on both.

The Foundation asked good questions and the chart confirms the field thinks so too. What it does not yet tell us is whether anyone can answer them, and that is the part worth watching.
